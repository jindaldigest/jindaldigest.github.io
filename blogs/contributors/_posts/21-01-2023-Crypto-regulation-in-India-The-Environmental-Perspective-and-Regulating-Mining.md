**Introduction**
The role of law and policy in the environment is a critical one. Law and policy can be used to protect the environment and have historically been used for the same. In many cases, these are the only tools we have to protect the environment. One way is to create regulations limiting the pollution released into the air, water, and soil or to promote sustainable practices. It is important to note that this may not always be effective in protecting the environment.
In some cases, they can be slow to respond to new threats, and they can also be influenced by lobbying from powerful industries. However, when used correctly, law and policy can be essential safeguarding mechanisms. India is in a crucial legislative period concerning cryptocurrency and its regulation (or a lack thereof). This article points out the issues with the seemingly low weightage given to environmental concerns when legislating on crypto in the Indian context.


**​Crypto’s carbon footprint**
It is well known that one of the significant drawbacks of cryptocurrency is the high carbon footprint of crypto mining. To mine, miners need to solve complex mathematical problems [(the ‘Proof of work’ model).](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.coinbase.com/learn/crypto-basics/what-is-proof-of-work-or-proof-of-stake) This involves a lot of computing power, which requires tremendous energy. In 2021, Tesla CEO Elon Musk stopped the acceptance of Bitcoin as a currency due to “[the rapidly increasing fossil fuels for Bitcoin mining](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/twitter.com/elonmusk/status/1392602041025843203?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw%7Ctwcamp%5Etweetembed%7Ctwterm%5E1392602041025843203%7Ctwgr%5E036f2b517628f9b07dbc8c9e9e3c48d955cb4898%7Ctwcon%5Es1_&ref_url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.cnbc.com%2F2021%2F05%2F12%2Felon-musk-says-tesla-will-stop-accepting-bitcoin-for-car-purchases.html)”. Ethereum, the second largest cryptocurrency by market capitalisation, had an overall electricity draw tallied at 23 million megawatt hours before 2022 (11 million tonnes [11,016,000] of CO2 Emissions, according to a [CCRI Report](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/carbon-ratings.com/dl/eth-report-2022)).
The high carbon footprint of crypto mining has led some to call for a more sustainable way of mining cryptocurrencies. One proposed solution is to use excess energy from renewable sources such as wind and solar. This extra energy is often wasted because the grid does not need it. However, it could be used to power cryptocurrency mining operations. Another proposed solution is to use blockchain technology to create a more efficient and decentralised power grid. This would allow for a more sustainable way of mining cryptocurrencies.
​
**The Ethereum Merge**
Ethereum herein becomes a focal point due to an important event on the blockchain that occurred in September 2022. Ethereum released the [‘Bellatrix upgrade’](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/blog.ethereum.org/2022/08/24/mainnet-merge-announcement), which merged the Ethereum blockchain with the Beacon chain, and turned it into a [‘Proof of stake’](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.forbes.com/sites/qai/2022/06/10/ethereums-big-day-what-the-merge-means-for-investors/?sh=2966c3411be3) model. (before this, Ethereum followed the ‘Proof of work’ model [“PoW”]). One significant upside of ‘The Merge’, as it has been termed, is a  drastic reduction in the carbon footprint of Ethereum. The Merge simply ensures that the consensus mechanism (the protocol/process that confirms or denies the need for a resource during computing) is less power intensive. As mentioned above, Ethereum generated 11 million tonnes of CO2 emissions. Post merge, that number currently stands at 869.78, a 99.992 % reduction in C02 emissions ([CCRI Report](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/carbon-ratings.com/dl/eth-report-2022)). This signals a pivot by market veterans to adopt more environmentally conscious and efficient consensus protocols.  

**Global trends**
In Europe, the Paris Agreement and its environmental obligations, act as a driving factor for regulating crypto. Authorities in the Nordic region, such as the Swedish Environmental Protection Agency and the Swedish Financial Supervisory Authority, have shown their [interest](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/financial-stability/macroprudential-bulletin/html/ecb.mpbu202207_3~d9614ea8e6.en.html) in instituting a ban on the PoW model of mining. In the United States, a similar situation has arisen wherein the government has taken cognizance of the environmental impact of ‘crypto assets’ and theorized a paradigm of ‘responsible development of digital assets’. The [report](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/09-2022-Crypto-Assets-and-Climate-Report.pdf) released by the White House tackles, in rich detail, the current/active impact of crypto assets and how to use them positively to help “Support Climate Monitoring or Mitigation”.

**Rethinking India’s stance on crypto from an environmental perspective**
India’s stance on cryptocurrency has remained largely chequered, dating back from the early 2010s when the popularity of crypto began to rise, till the current day. No strict regulations are in place- only RBI circulars in the form of warnings, including one that banned the trade, buying and mining of crypto entirely, which has been [set aside](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.thehindu.com/news/national/supreme-court-sets-aside-rbi-ban-on-cryptocurrency-transactions/article61967124.ece) by the apex court. Therefore, there is a lacuna in what little ‘regulation’ exists. Several bills have also aimed to regulate and/or entirely ban cryptocurrencies in India. A common theme, however, emerges when one analyses past introduced crypto regulation bills. There is no constructive engagement with the environmental impact of crypto mining.
As mentioned above, crypto’s environmental impact is significant and must be taken seriously. There has been a fleeting mention in an [IMC Report](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/dea.gov.in/sites/default/files/Approved%20and%20Signed%20Report%20and%20Bill%20of%20IMC%20on%20VCs%2028%20Feb%202019.pdf) (2019), which, through its 108 pages, only mentions the environmental impact of crypto in one brief sentence- “The mining of non-official virtual currencies is very resource intensive.” However, the dominating arguments for the ban or regulation of crypto have restricted themselves to the economic sphere.
In Indian legal discourse and regulatory circles, levying sizeable taxes is the current [standard](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.forbes.com/advisor/in/tax/how-cryptocurrencies-are-taxed-in-india/). However, the environmental aspect needs recognition. We will likely see a significant rise in mining activity due to the cut of computational power required with the switch to the PoS model. This does not mean that mining will go away entirely. Miners may switch to PoW-based crypto like Ethereum’s PoW chain, Ergo etc. Crypto mining, thus, needs to be regulated to serve the interests and choices of the miners, as it is [estimated](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.thehindubusinessline.com/info-tech/winter-is-coming-for-crypto-miners-in-india/article65866041.ece) that miners earn between $35-50 and can earn bonuses based on their mining setup’s performance in generating tokens. This demonstrates an industry-based need.
Implementing regulations requiring miners to disclose their energy usage and sources effectively promotes transparency and accountability in the cryptocurrency industry. This can be achieved through reporting requirements and audits, where miners must acquire licenses and submit regular reports detailing their energy consumption and sources, which government agencies or independent third-party organizations then audit. For instance, in the United States, the state of New York has regulated crypto mining. The ‘[BitLicense’](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.usatoday.com/story/tech/2015/06/03/bitcoin-bitlicense-lawsky-rules-final/28405317/) is a business license issued by the New York Department of Financial Services (DFS), a regulatory body that oversees banks like Goldman Sachs and Barclays. The license and compliance requirements stipulate minimum thresholds for conducting virtual currency activities. Companies engaging in said activities must appoint compliance personnel for BitLicense approvals and concerns. Similar license-based legislation in India can help formalise the industry and ensure any future economic benefits are not lost.
Encouraging mining through friendly regulation makes sense from a stakeholder perspective and an ease-of-doing business standpoint. Mining is a highly resource-intensive process. A [study](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/cointelegraph.com/news/study-crypto-mining-consumes-more-energy-than-mineral-mining) from 2018 says that crypto-mining consumes more energy than mineral mining. Kazakhstan, a mining haven, [recently](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.coindesk.com/policy/2022/12/19/kazakhstan-tightens-regulation-for-miners-looks-to-develop-broader-crypto-industry/) introduced new regulatory policies to standardise mining practices in a resource-capped environment and help broaden the crypto industry’s penetration. Kazakhstan continues to rely on its Soviet-era infrastructure to feed its electricity needs. The new regulation aims to allocate 500 megawatts of electricity to miners. This allocation is, however, restricted to instances when the national grid has excess production. India is relevant in this conversation due to its push for renewable energy. Unlike Kazakhstan’s minimal investment in setting up green energy infrastructure, India is a global leader in green energy investment, with $14.5 billion invested in [2021-22](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.weforum.org/agenda/2022/07/india-investment-renewables-green-energy/). A Load Generation Balance Report ([LGBR](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/mercomindia.com/india-likely-have-energy-surplus-during-2022-23-cea/)) by India’s Central Electricity Authority (CEA) has indicated that India will have a 3.4 per cent peak surplus in 2022-23. This surplus translates into 7.4 gigawatts of surplus available electricity. The CEA has also [claimed](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/qz.com/2088411/should-india-use-its-burgeoning-green-energy-for-crypto-mining) that renewable sources shall generate 280 gigawatts of electricity by 2030. Renewable infrastructure like photovoltaic farms and surplus energy allocation may help formulate a miner-friendly regulation for crypto assets. Surplus energy could be commodified by allocating ‘energy credits’ to licensed or registered mining outfits, thereby cutting down on unauthorised electricity usage or dangerous mining infrastructure from being set up. Kazakhstan also [requires](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/cointelegraph.com/news/kazakhstan-ramps-up-power-consumption-reporting-requirements-for-crypto-miners) comprehensive information on technical specifications and other reporting requirements before authorising miners.
The recent [amendment](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.mca.gov.in/bin/ebook/dms/getdocument?doc=MjU4MzU=&docCategory=Others&type=open) to the Companies Act in 2021 requires companies to disclose profit or loss, the amount held, and deposits related to cryptocurrencies. Integrating policies to check mining, especially at an institutional level in line with the 2021 Companies Act amendment, which requires companies to track and disclose carbon footprint related to mining, is crucial to keeping a check on environmental impacts. Creating such reporting requirements for mining infrastructure is also important because crypto mining could qualify as *abstraction* (dishonestly using, wasting or diverting energy)  under the Indian Electricity Act of 1910, which is a penal offence.

**Conclusion**
India has applied a 1 per cent TDS (in addition to a 30 per cent tax on transfers) on crypto. One could argue that this is a step back in decentralisation; we refrain from assessing the viability of a tax regime for crypto as many prominent crypto havens are also recircling on this question. However, the quantum of taxation needs rethinking due to uncertainty about the technology’s potential and uses. Mining, for instance, is a grey area with many uncertainties presently.
Authorities need to consider the financial prospects of investing in crypto/blockchain projects that can help with socio-economic development through peer-to-peer deliverables, as NITI Aayog has already noted in a strategy [paper](https://web.archive.org/web/20230606075011/https:/www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2020-01/Blockchain_The_India_Strategy_Part_I.pdf). Crypto miners and stakeholders are involved in an intricate process of developing and maintaining critical infrastructure for a fledgling industry, i.e., the blockchain industry.
